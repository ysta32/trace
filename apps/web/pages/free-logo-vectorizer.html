Start with the best version of your logo

A logo needs to work on a small profile picture, a presentation slide, and a large printed sign. A raster copy can become visibly pixelated as it grows. Vector paths describe its contours independently of resolution, making SVG useful for resizing and editing. Trace helps rebuild those contours from a source image while keeping processing on your device through WebAssembly.

Before tracing, look for an existing vector original. A designer's SVG, EPS, or vector PDF will usually preserve the intended geometry more accurately than an automatic reconstruction. When only a bitmap remains, choose the cleanest and largest version available. A flat logo on an uncluttered background is generally a better source than a photograph of a business card.

Build a usable vector logo in five steps

  1. Open the source image. Keep an untouched copy for reference. If possible, prepare a crop containing just the logo in your image editor before bringing it into Trace.
  2. Start with the logo preset. It gives you a starting point for flat artwork. Use the line art preset for a primarily drawn mark; choose based on the actual image rather than its filename.
  3. Check the detected colors. Automatic palette and color count detection can get you started. Compare the result with the brand palette and edit colors deliberately instead of treating sampled pixels as authoritative.
  4. Inspect the mark and lettering. Use the split-slider preview to compare boundaries. Select and recolor individual shapes, or delete unwanted specks after confirming they are not part of the design.
  5. Export and proof. Save SVG for further editing, then view it at the smallest and largest sizes you expect to use.

What to check before calling a logo finished

Pay special attention to the spaces inside letters, the ends of thin strokes, and repeated geometric features. Small asymmetries can become obvious when a mark is enlarged. Automatic tracing does not know which circles were meant to be identical or which gaps must match. For exact brand geometry, use a vector editor to make final corrections against a trusted reference.

Lettering becomes outlined shapes, not a recovered font or editable text object. If the wordmark needs a spelling change, find the original typeface and rebuild the text in a design tool. Avoid stretching the whole logo to fit a new layout: scaling proportionally preserves the relationship between its elements.

A raster image may contain slightly different shades along otherwise solid edges. Reducing the palette can remove unnecessary complexity, but an aggressive reduction can also erase small accents. Check a one-color version separately if you need it for stamps or simple printing. Do not assume the full-color trace will automatically meet that production requirement.

From one logo to a reusable collection

Trace exports SVG, PDF, EPS, DXF, and PNG, so you can choose a format suited to the receiving workflow. Confirm print dimensions and color handling with your printer. A DXF export still needs inspection in the intended CAD or cutting software; a traced logo is not automatically a validated toolpath.

Batch processing is useful for related assets, but review each logo individually. Developers can use the CLI or install npm i trace-vectorizer for repeatable workflows. The project is open source under MIT. The software license does not grant rights to someone else's logo, so use artwork you own or have permission to adapt.

Frequently asked questions

Is logo vectorization free?

Yes. Trace is free to use and runs image processing locally in your browser. You can refine the traced artwork and export the result.

Can it restore a blurry logo perfectly?

No. Missing details cannot be recovered with certainty. A larger source or manual reconstruction may be necessary for precise lettering and brand geometry.

Which format should I give a designer?

SVG is a useful starting point for editable vector paths. Ask the designer whether their workflow calls for SVG, PDF, or EPS, and include the original reference image.
